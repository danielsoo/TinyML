\begin{abstract}
Federated intrusion detectors are trained on server-class hardware but must run on microcontrollers
with tens of kilobytes of memory. We ask when compressing such a detector to INT8 preserves its
usefulness at a fixed false-alarm rate, why it fails when it does, and which remedies work when only
client-held data are available. On \cic and \ton, with a data pipeline audited for client ownership
and test isolation, thresholds chosen for a 1\% false-alarm budget on client validation data, and
three independent federated runs, the best 57--65\,KB INT8 models detect 98.5\% and 99.6\% of test
attacks at an achieved test false-alarm rate of 1.16\% and 0.92\%. A compact model trained federatively
from scratch matches the complete recovery recipe (distillation, clipped inputs, QAT) on \cic and on
unseen hosts and trails it by 0.5--1.6 points on \ton. Failures have one dominant cause, the input
quantization range: ten extreme records in a 2,000-record calibration draw drive recall to 0\%, and
quantization-aware training (QAT) fails for the same reason because it learns that range from
unclipped inputs. Bounding the input range removes both failures at no device cost, although clipping
saturates features of 8--28\% of test records. On hosts never seen in training, the compact INT8 models
lose 1.6--4.4 points of recall relative to the float teacher. The residual loss lies in coarse
activations near the operating point and is recovered on the host by 16-bit activations. Finally, the
exact exported file and runtime decide what the device does: on TFLM 1.0.0 the per-tensor INT8 models
reproduce the host decisions on an ESP32-S3 and an ESP32 (5.6--6.5 and 13.2--15.4\,ms per flow), while
the converter's default per-channel export silently disagrees on half of its decisions; the current
Espressif runtime restores agreement for the per-channel model we tested, and the 16-bit model runs
correctly on neither runtime.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{federated learning, TinyML, intrusion detection, quantization, microcontrollers}

\section{Introduction}
Intrusion detection on IoT devices is increasingly learned from traffic that clients do not want to
pool~\cite{atzori2010iot}. Federated learning (FL) trains a shared model from client
updates~\cite{mcmahan2017communication,konecny2016federated}, and TinyML runs integer models on
microcontrollers~\cite{banbury2020benchmarking,david2021tflm}. Putting the two together requires a
step that is easy to state and easy to get wrong: the federated model has to be compressed, usually
to INT8 and a fraction of its size, without pooled data, and the compressed model has to keep
detecting attacks at the false-alarm rate an operator can tolerate.

Published federated and TinyML intrusion detectors report accuracy or F1 at a fixed threshold, on
splits whose preprocessing often sees the test data, and rarely check the device. We found all three
choices in our own earlier pipeline: oversampling before the client partition, thresholds tuned on
synthetic records, and a test split that guided method development (\S\ref{sec:design}). After
rebuilding the pipeline we ask three questions:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] When does compression preserve useful detection, under memory budgets of 80 and 128\,KB
  and false-alarm (FAR) budgets of 1\% and 5\% chosen on client validation data?
\item[RQ2] Why does it fail when it does?
\item[RQ3] Which remedies work under memory and client-data constraints, and what do they cost on the device?
\end{description}
On \cic and \ton with three federated runs per configuration, the answers form three connected findings:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A compact model trained from scratch is often enough (RQ1, RQ3).} At 57--65\,KB, a model
  trained federatively at the target size (S80) matches the complete teacher-based recovery recipe R
  (pruning, distilled fine-tuning on clipped inputs, QAT on clipped inputs) on \cic and on unseen hosts;
  R is better on the random \ton split by 0.5--1.6 points in every run, at a 0.1--0.2-point higher
  achieved test FAR. Both have the same architecture, size and latency; they differ in training cost
  (\S\ref{sec:rq1}, Table~\ref{tab:deploy}).
\item \textbf{The input quantization range causes and prevents the failures (RQ2, RQ3).} A controlled
  2$\times$2 isolates the input range, not hidden ranges, as the cause of collapses; the same mechanism
  explains why QAT fails; bounding the range (clipped or extreme-free calibration, QAT on clipped inputs)
  removes the failures at no device cost, with an accuracy trade-off for models that use extreme
  inputs. The remaining loss comes from tie groups of coarse INT8 outputs and is recovered on the
  host, not on the device, by 16-bit activations (\S\ref{sec:rq2}, \S\ref{sec:rq3}).
\item \textbf{Detection must be verified for the exact exported model and runtime (\S\ref{sec:device}).}
  On TFLM 1.0.0 the per-tensor INT8 models reproduce the host decisions on two ESP32 chips, while
  per-channel exports of the same models silently do not. On the classic ESP32 the current Espressif
  runtime ran the tested per-channel model correctly and gave bit-identical outputs for its per-tensor
  counterpart; the 16-bit model ran correctly on neither runtime.
\end{itemize}
We also contribute the audited protocol itself: client-owned partitions before any fitted step,
real per-client validation slices, FAR-budget thresholds applied once to a test split that was excluded
from training, threshold selection and calibration of every reported model (its records had been in the
training pool of our earlier, exploratory pipeline; \S\ref{sec:data}), and an unseen-host split. Per-run
results, paired differences, device disagreement classes, resource breakdowns and stream-completeness
records are in the supplementary material (S1--S8).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\paragraph{Datasets.} \cic~\cite{sharafaldin2018toward} is the most used flow-based IDS benchmark;
Engelen et al.~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting} document labelling and flow-construction errors in it.
\ton~\cite{moustafa2021toniot} and Bot-IoT~\cite{koroniotis2019botiot} target IoT traffic.

\paragraph{Federated and lightweight intrusion detection.} D\"IoT~\cite{nguyen2019diot} trains
per-device-type detectors with FL; Belarbi et al.~\cite{belarbi2023federated} use one \ton client per
IP address; FLAME~\cite{nguyen2022flame} defends FL against backdoors. Li et al.~\cite{li2025cognitive}
combine FL and TinyML for intrusion detection on N-BaIoT with feature reduction and quantization.
Kitsune~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}, HorusEye~\cite{dong2023horuseye} and NetBeacon~\cite{zhou2023netbeacon}
run detectors on gateways or switches; we target the endpoint microcontroller. We use
FedAvgM~\cite{hsu2019measuring} in Flower~\cite{beutel2020flower}.

\paragraph{TinyML and compression.} TFLM~\cite{david2021tflm} runs integer models on
microcontrollers; MCUNet~\cite{lin2020mcunet}, on-device training~\cite{lin2022ondevice},
SeeDot~\cite{gopinath2019seedot} and MinUn~\cite{jaiswal2023minun} co-design models and runtimes.
HeteroFL~\cite{diao2021heterofl} and FedTiny~\cite{huang2023fedtiny} produce small models during
federated training; we compress a trained federated model or train a compact one from scratch.
Pruning~\cite{han2016deep}, distillation~\cite{hinton2015distilling,polino2018model} and
quantization~\cite{jacob2018quantization,gholami2021survey,nagel2021white} are standard; QAT can
oscillate~\cite{nagel2022overcoming}, and pruned weights re-densify under standard
QAT~\cite{tfmot2021collab}. In FL, quantization mostly compresses updates~\cite{reisizadeh2020fedpaq};
Gupta et al.\ train models robust to later quantization~\cite{gupta2023quantization}.

\paragraph{Calibration ranges.} Percentile and KL calibration~\cite{wu2020integer} and the MSE-optimal
clip of ACIQ~\cite{banner2019aciq} clip activation ranges instead of using extremes;
PACT~\cite{choi2018pact} and LSQ~\cite{esser2020lsq} learn the clip or step during QAT, and
DFQ~\cite{nagel2019dfq} calibrates without data. These methods target hidden activations; we find
that for tabular flow features the \emph{input} range decides the outcome. Deployment faults that
yield wrong models without errors are common~\cite{chen2021deployment,jajal2024onnx}; \S\ref{sec:device}
adds one for microcontroller runtimes.

\section{Experimental Design}\label{sec:design}
\subsection{Data, Audit and Splits}\label{sec:data}
We use the eight \cic CSV files (78 features, binary label) and the \ton network train/test file
(37 numeric and label-encoded fields plus six length / digit / symbol counts from the DNS query and
URI). Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the audited pipeline. An audit of our earlier pipeline
(Appendix~\ref{app:audit}) found that SMOTE ran on the pooled training set before the client partition
(37.5\% / 35.1\% synthetic rows, with synthetic descendants of one client's records on others), that
the validation slice used to choose thresholds was part of a client's training data and 74.6\% (\cic
attack rows) / 71.0\% (\ton benign rows) synthetic, that 4.7\% of synthetic \ton rows carried values
impossible for their label-encoded fields, and that the test split had guided method choices. All
results in this paper come from the rebuilt pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
\item exact duplicates are removed and feature vectors that occur with both labels are dropped; on
  \cic, 2,926 records with impossible negative values (overflowed header lengths down to
  $-3.2\cdot10^{10}$, negative durations and inter-arrival times) are dropped as data errors, while
  the defined $-1$ sentinel of \texttt{Init\_Win\_bytes} is kept;
\item the test split (20\%) is drawn first, only from records that were not in the earlier test split;
\item records are assigned to four clients (label-balanced) before any fitted step; each client holds
  out a real, stratified 10\% validation slice and undersamples its own training slice to at most 4:1;
\item the scaler uses the mean and variance of the union of client training slices, which a
  federation can aggregate from per-client sums; validation and test are never used.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Test isolation and freeze history.} All revised test records come from the training pool of our
earlier, exploratory pipeline: models of that pipeline saw them in training and guided our hypotheses,
but none of the records was in the earlier test split (Supplement S7a). They are excluded from training,
threshold selection and calibration of every reported model, and the record ids and hashes of every
partition are stored per run and checked before evaluation. The pipeline, arms, budgets, threshold rule
and calibration candidates were committed before any revised result, and the rules that make the test
split exclude the earlier test records before any revised test result. Added after test results, and
marked as post hoc: the QC/SQC arms, the clipped-FP32 and 16-bit rows of E4, the unseen-host split, the
per-tensor re-exports and the runtime comparison (time line in Supplement S7b).

For a second test condition (E6) we split \ton by source host: all records of 15 of 51 source IPs form
the test split, so no test host is seen in training. \cic carries no host field in its CSVs.

\subsection{Federated Training}\label{sec:fltrain}
All models are MLPs with ReLU, post-ReLU BatchNorm (folded exactly into the next layer before
compression, Appendix~\ref{app:bn}) and dropout, trained with Flower and FedAvgM (server momentum 0.5,
server learning rate 0.1) for 60 rounds of 3 local epochs, Adam with cosine decay from $10^{-3}$ to
$10^{-4}$, class weights and focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal} ($\alpha=0.35$ on \cic, 0.5 on \ton). The
teacher F is 512-256-128. Compact models trained from scratch with the same recipe are S80
(256-128-64, 57\,KB / 65\,KB as INT8) and S128 (358-179-89, 100\,KB / 112\,KB). Every configuration is
trained with seeds 1, 2 and 3 (E5); a run takes 3--4\,min on \ton and 26--41\,min on \cic on one PC.

\subsection{Compared Arms}\label{sec:arms}
For each teacher seed and memory budget (80\,KB: structured pruning keeps 50\% of the neurons;
128\,KB: 70\%) we compare:
\begin{description}
\item[F / F8] the float teacher, and the teacher with strong PTQ (189 / 207\,KB, over budget; a reference);
\item[P] prune $\rightarrow$ fine-tune 3 FedAvg rounds on the clients' data $\rightarrow$ strong PTQ;
\item[Q] the same pruned and fine-tuned model $\rightarrow$ QAT for 2 rounds $\rightarrow$ INT8;
\item[QC] as Q, but QAT on inputs clipped to $\pm$5 standard deviations;
\item[R] prune $\rightarrow$ fine-tune distilled from F (temperature 2, $\alpha=0.5$) on clipped
  inputs $\rightarrow$ QAT on clipped inputs $\rightarrow$ INT8;
\item[S / SQ / SQC] the from-scratch compact model with strong PTQ / QAT / QAT on clipped inputs.
\end{description}
\emph{Strong PTQ} builds seven calibrations from 2,000 records of client 0 (TFLite min/max with and
without $\pm$5\,SD input clipping, per-tensor 99.9 / 99.99th percentile, MSE~\cite{banner2019aciq},
KL~\cite{wu2020integer}, clipped 99.99th percentile) and keeps the one with the best mean validation
recall at the two FAR budgets; reported F8, P and S80 models are restricted to the per-tensor candidates, the form that TFLM 1.0.0 executes correctly (\S\ref{sec:device}).
Fine-tuning uses 10k records per client, either FedAvg over all four
clients or one client alone (the client-data constraint).

\subsection{Operating Points and Metrics}\label{sec:metrics}
For every model and stage, the threshold is the smallest one whose FAR on the pooled client validation
slices is at most the budget (1\% or 5\%). It is frozen and applied once to the test split; we report
attack recall and the \emph{achieved} test FAR, mean $\pm$ SD over the three teacher seeds. The budget
constrains validation FAR only; test FAR can exceed it (on \ton it reaches 1.27\% at the 1\% budget) and,
on unseen hosts, differs from it by several points (\S\ref{sec:rq1}). Decision transitions (E2)
compare each test attack's decision at consecutive stages (teacher, compact FP32, QAT float, INT8),
each at its own frozen threshold. Thresholds are computed in float32: with NumPy 1.x a float64
threshold is cast back to float32 in the comparison, which silently re-admits a whole tie group of
INT8 outputs and let validation FAR reach 44\% at a 5\% budget in our first run.

\paragraph{Compression preserves detection when the input range is bounded.} Every arm whose input
range is bounded (P with strong PTQ, QC, R, S80, SQC) lies within one SD of the teacher's recall at the
1\% budget or above it; the means of R, QC, S80 and SQC exceed the teacher's on both datasets, at achieved
test FARs of 0.92--1.16\% against the teacher's 0.84--0.98\%. The 128\,KB budget adds at most 1.2 points
over 80\,KB. The two QAT arms that learn their input range from unclipped data (Q, SQ) fail;
\S\ref{sec:rq2} explains why.

\paragraph{The float teacher's 1\% operating point is the least stable.} F has the largest SD (1.4 and
2.1 points). On \cic about 2,265 PortScan flows per run sit just below its 1\% threshold, and every
compact or INT8 model recovers them (\S\ref{sec:e2}). This is an operating-point sensitivity of the
teacher, not a benefit of compression (\S\ref{sec:e4}).

\section{RQ2: Why Compression Fails}\label{sec:rq2}
\subsection{Where Decisions Change (E2)}\label{sec:e2}
Table~\ref{tab:e2} counts the test attacks whose decision changes between stages. Pruning and
fine-tuning move thousands of decisions in both directions; the INT8 conversion moves few, except
when the input range is wrong. For Q the loss happens in QAT itself, before conversion: 45,603 attacks
(three runs) are lost between the compact FP32 and the QAT float model and 15 in the conversion. R's
conversion loses 127 attacks where P's loses 1,040 (Figure~\ref{fig:e2}): P's losses concentrate in
the small web-attack families (brute force 360, XSS 168) and DoS Hulk, which R keeps; S80 loses mostly
DoS Hulk and Bot flows. Two of P's three largest losses are among the smallest families (1,507 web

\subsection{Output and Activation Resolution (E4)}\label{sec:e4}
Table~\ref{tab:e4} separates the remaining INT8 effects. Rounding only the FP32 output to the INT8
output grid (step 1/256) costs at most 0.25 points: the output grid is not the problem. The INT8 models,
however, produce few distinct outputs on the validation slices (23--35 for the pruned \cic models vs.\
241--245 for the rounded FP32 output), so up to about 1,000 benign validation records share the output
value just below the threshold. The 1\% budget can then only be met by jumping over the whole tie group
(validation FAR 0.74\% instead of 1\%), which costs recall. Exporting the logit instead of the sigmoid
gives identical decisions: the bottleneck is the INT8 logit and the activations before it. With 16-bit
activations (an exported int16x8 model: int16 activations, per-channel int8 weights, int64 biases, run by
the host interpreter) the tie group shrinks to about 10 records and most of the loss returns (\cic: 462
lost attacks become 131). This recovery is a host result: no device runtime we tested executed the
16-bit model correctly (\S\ref{sec:device}). On \ton, where INT8 gains over FP32, clipping the FP32 inputs to
$\pm$5\,SD gives the same gain: it is input saturation. On \cic the INT8 gain of F and S80 (+2.1--2.5 points,
the PortScan cluster of \S\ref{sec:rq1}) disappears with 16-bit activations and is not reproduced by
clipped FP32 inputs: INT8 rounding moves those flows across the teacher's threshold, an operating-point
effect rather than a robust improvement.

\section{RQ3: Remedies and Their Cost}\label{sec:rq3}
Table~\ref{tab:remedies} lists what worked, its effect and its cost. Bounding the input range is the
remedy that matters: it is free on the device, needs no extra client data, and turns the two failure
modes (min/max calibration with extreme records, QAT on unclipped inputs) into the best arms.
Clipping is not free in accuracy: $\pm$5\,SD saturates at least one feature in 28\% of \cic and 7.6\% of
\ton test records, and costs the \cic teacher 1.5 points against extreme-free min/max calibration, which is
the gentler variant for models that use the tails. The complete recovery recipe R (distilled fine-tuning
on clipped inputs, then QAT on clipped inputs) improves on P by 1.9 points on \ton and 0.5 on \cic. We did
not isolate its components: QC, which has the clipped QAT but no distillation, is within 0.65 points of R
on \ton and 0.03 on \cic, so we attribute the gain to the recipe as a whole. Training a compact model from
scratch removes the compression step altogether at the price of a second federated training
(Table~\ref{tab:deploy}). 16-bit activations remove most of the remaining tie loss on the host, but no
tested device runtime executed the 16-bit model correctly, so their deployment cost is not established.
In our label-balanced partition, one client's 10k records sufficed for fine-tuning.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:limits}
\emph{Scope}: two datasets, binary detection, one model family (MLP) and four label-balanced clients; the
one-client result holds for this partition only. \emph{Splits}: the revised test records were in the
training pool of our earlier, exploratory pipeline, whose models guided our hypotheses; they were excluded
from training, threshold selection and calibration of every reported model, and several analyses were
added after test results (\S\ref{sec:data}). \cic has no host field, so the unseen-host test is \ton-only,
and its benign test set comes from few hosts, making its test FAR depend on single clusters.
\emph{Seeds}: three federated runs per configuration; the float teacher's 1\%-FAR recall varies by up to
2 points across runs, and claims about it are stated as sensitivity. \emph{Attribution}: we did not
isolate the components of the recovery recipe R. \emph{Labels}: \cic contains labelling
errors~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting}; we drop impossible values and conflicting labels but do not
relabel. \emph{Runtime}: TFLM 1.0.0 on two ESP32 chips for all device models; the current esp-tflite-micro
1.4.2 only for three files on the classic ESP32, so its behaviour on the other models and on the ESP32-S3
(where esp-nn's optimized kernels apply) is not established, and the cause of the 16-bit failure is not
diagnosed. \emph{Threat model}: no adversarial or poisoning study~\cite{nguyen2022flame}.

\section{Conclusion}
A federated intrusion detector can be compressed to 57--65\,KB INT8 and keep its detection at a 1\%
validation false-alarm budget on two datasets and three runs, using only client-held data, at achieved
test false-alarm rates of 0.9--1.2\%; on unseen hosts the compact models lose 1.6--4.4 points of recall
relative to the float teacher. A compact model trained federatively from scratch is a sufficient choice on
\cic and on unseen hosts, while the complete recovery recipe gives a consistent gain on the random \ton
split. When compression fails, the input quantization range is the cause, for post-training quantization
and QAT alike, and bounding it prevents the failure at no device cost. The remaining loss sits in coarse
activations at the operating point; 16-bit activations recover it on the host only. Finally, the exact
exported file and runtime decide what the device does: under TFLM 1.0.0 per-tensor INT8 models agree with
the host on both chips and per-channel exports do not, the current Espressif runtime restored agreement for
the per-channel model we tested on the classic ESP32, and the 16-bit model ran correctly on neither.

\section{Exact BatchNorm Folding}\label{app:bn}
Each hidden layer is Dense$\rightarrow$ReLU$\rightarrow$BatchNorm$\rightarrow$Dropout. BatchNorm at
inference is $s\odot h+t$ with $s=\gamma/\sqrt{\sigma^2+\varepsilon}$ and $t=\beta-s\odot\mu$. Since it
follows the ReLU it is folded into the next Dense layer $(W,b)$: $W'=\mathrm{diag}(s)W$, $b'=b+t^{\top}W$
(deviation $\le2\cdot10^{-5}$). Folding it into the preceding layer, a common shortcut, changed 11.6\% of
decisions on our layout.

\section{Toolchain Pitfalls}\label{app:pitfalls}
Six failures produced plausible numbers without any error: int8 weight exchange without scales in FL;
QAT-wrapper stripping that copied no weights; BatchNorm folded on the wrong side; pruning that reset
BatchNorm statistics; float64 thresholds compared with float32 INT8 outputs (\S\ref{sec:metrics}); and
per-channel exports executed incorrectly, without any error, by an older microcontroller runtime (\S\ref{sec:device}). The checks that
catch them: dequantize-requantize equality at the receiver, copy-by-name with output comparison,
equivalence assertions after folding, validation FAR asserted against the budget, and device streaming
against the host.
